L1 Rocket

University of Auckland Rocketry Club

L1 Rocket is a 60cm single stage rocket built for the University of Auckland Rocketry Club's first Level 1 certification flight. On an AeroTech H128W, OpenRocket simulation suggests an apogee of 558m and Mach 0.44, with 2 calibers of stability. I built the rocket with one other team member, carrying the design from the OpenRocket model through a Fusion 360 assembly into a finished airframe. The rocket is fully built and waiting on a launch window.

Why we are building it

Level 1 is the entry certification for high power rocketry. A candidate earns it by flying a rocket on an H or I class motor and recovering it in a flyable state, observed by a certifying official. I am pursuing the certification for two reasons. It grants access to the larger motor classes, and it would allow me to contribute to the more powerful rockets the club intends to build. That objective shaped the airframe, which is why the design remains simple. The rocket is single stage with motor ejection and a single parachute, and carries nothing that introduces a further failure mode.

The certification requirement fixed the motor class, so we sized the airframe around it. We iterated tube length, nose cone profile and fin planform in OpenRocket until the centre of pressure sat two calibers, or two body diameters, behind the centre of gravity. A margin below roughly one caliber leaves a rocket liable to go unstable off the rail, while an excessive margin causes it to weathercock into the wind and give up altitude. Two calibers sits at the conservative end of the usual range, which suits a flight where recovering the airframe intact matters more than reaching altitude.

I modelled each component on its own in Fusion 360, the nose cone and its shoulder, the body tube, the inner motor mount tube, the centering rings, and the fin set, before bringing them together into a single assembly. The assembly then exposed the fits that are expensive to correct once material has been cut, in particular the centering rings between the inner and outer tubes and the shoulder against the body tube bore. The fin profile came out of the model as a flat pattern and went straight to the laser cutter.

Manufacturing was distributed across the equipment available to us. I printed the nose cone on my home 3D printer, and the club supplied the shoulder that joins it to the airframe. We laser cut the fins at the university and cut the inner and outer tubes to length by hand with a saw. Assembly is entirely epoxy. We bonded the centering rings to the inner tube, the shoulder to the nose cone, and the fins into the body tube. Fin alignment demanded the most care of any step, since a fin set that sits out of square induces roll and gives up altitude. Recovery is a single parachute released by the motor ejection charge, with the six second delay chosen so that it fires near apogee rather than at speed.

What I took from it

The build decided more of the design than the simulation did. A fin planform that looks fine in OpenRocket still has to be cut, aligned by eye against a line drawn on a cardboard tube, and hold together at 150 m/s.

Skills used. OpenRocket simulation, stability and margin analysis, Fusion 360 modelling, design for manufacture, 3D printing, laser cutting, drilling, epoxy bonding and fin alignment.

What is left before flight

  1. Fit the rail buttons
  2. Sew the parachute
  3. Paint the airframe
  4. Fly the rocket and recover it in a flyable state

The rocket carries no onboard instrumentation, so the flight will be judged on a straight boost and an intact recovery rather than on measured altitude. The 558m prediction stays a prediction. Adding an altimeter is the first change I would make to the next airframe, since it turns the simulation into something we can check.
