Warman Competition

Group design project • University of Auckland design paper

A compact three arm autonomous robot built for the Warman competition, which required a machine to collect two tennis ball meteorites sitting at different heights, cross an unstable ramp, and deposit both balls into a bunker within 60 seconds. The whole robot had to fit inside a 300 mm cube at the start of every run. I worked in a team of four and owned two subsystems, the central deposition system that received the balls and guided them into the bunker, and the software that ran the autonomous sequence. The finished robot scored approximately 117 points, completed a run in around 12 seconds, and finished the full sequence on more than 95 percent of test runs.

Purpose

The scoring rewarded finishing the task rather than attempting it. Depositing both meteorites was worth up to 60 points, using the ramp added 10, and every second saved under a minute added one more. A team that deposited both balls quickly could clear 100 points, while a team that only knocked a ball off the stand scored 5. That gap set the priority for the whole build. We chose mechanical simplicity over clever control and gave each stage of the task its own dedicated mechanism, so that no single component was asked to do more than one job. My two subsystems sat at opposite ends of that decision. The deposition system had to work by geometry alone with no actuator of its own, and the software had to run the sequence without any of the four drive and arm motors fighting each other for hardware.

My priority through the design was to make every part printable flat with no support material. Supports cost print time on a project where I was reprinting parts between test sessions, and they scar the surface they touch, which matters on a ramp a ball has to roll down. Printing the back wall and the ramps as one piece could not be done without overhangs, so I broke the system into modular parts that each lie flat on the bed and join with slot features. Splitting it that way also meant that when a ramp angle needed changing after testing, I reprinted one small part overnight rather than the whole assembly.

The printed back wall had to slot onto a 3 mm MDF base and hold there under vibration without glue, and a printer that is a fraction out will give either a slot that will not go on or one that rattles. Rather than print the wall and find out, I printed a single test coupon with a row of cutouts stepped just above and below 3 mm, pushed the MDF into each one, and used the width that gave a tight friction fit. That number then went into every part in the assembly that touched the base. It took one short print to learn what my printer actually produces against what the model says, and it is the reason the assembly fitted first time.

Once the arms lift a ball over the chassis, nothing actively controls where it goes. I designed the ramps and the cylindrical funnel that catch the balls and route them down into the bunker, which meant the geometry had to do all of the work. The funnel connects to a cylindrical guide that drops through the chassis and lands the ball cleanly in the hole, and the guide also walls the ball off from the internal wiring that runs through the same space. Wiring that gets knocked by a 60g ball on every run becomes a loose connection later, and loose connections were already causing intermittent failures elsewhere on the robot.

I wrote the Arduino code that runs the whole run start to finish, pushing the ramp down with the servo arm, lowering and raising the collection arms, reversing until the limit switch hits the wall, then following the line until the bunker sensor ends the run. The problem that took the longest was a hardware limit rather than a logic error. Driving four DC motors at controlled speed needs PWM on several pins at once, and the Arduino Servo library takes over the timer those pins depend on, so the motors and the servo could not run in the same program. I removed the library and generated the servo pulses directly in code, which freed the timers and gave the motors back their channels. The line following itself is a proportional correction on the difference between two sensor readings, kept simple on purpose so a mistimed step could not lose a run.

What I took from it

Good engineering means designing around the real limits of the hardware and the manufacturing process rather than the ideal case. Two of the three problems worth writing about here came from that gap. The slot widths in CAD were not the slot widths my printer produced, and the Arduino could not run the motors and the servo at once no matter how the sequence was written. Neither shows up on screen. The team learned the same thing from the other direction on the collection arms, where four physical iterations were built before anyone did the moment analysis that would have sized the gear ratio in an afternoon. Prototyping and analysis each catch what the other misses.

Skills used. Fusion 360 part and assembly modelling, design for manufacture, support free print design, tolerance testing and friction fits, 3D printing in PLA, laser cut MDF assembly, Arduino programming in C++.
