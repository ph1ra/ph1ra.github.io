Milk Cap

Individual design project

Silicone Milk Cap is a reusable, squeeze-to-open replacement for the twist cap on a 2L milk bottle, designed for café use and moulded from food-safe silicone. It comes off with a squeeze rather than a twist. Pinching two wings on the sides stretches the ring out of round and lifts two hooks off the bottle neck. A strap keeps it attached to the bottle handle. I designed it individually, carrying it from user requirements through SolidWorks and a 3D printed mould to two moulded and tested prototypes.

Why I decided to design this

A milk bottle cap is something a barista handles dozens of times a shift, one-handed, with a jug in the other hand and cold wet fingers. I hold a barista certificate and make coffee daily at home, so the twist cap is an annoyance I know first-hand. Café staff were the users I chose to design for, and that gave me somewhere concrete to start.

Two soft wings sit on the sides of the cap. Squeezing them ovalizes the shape, which lifts two small hooks off the bottle neck so the cap comes straight off. A strap holds it to the bottle handle so it never ends up on the bench.

I chose this over a flip lid or a valve because the whole thing can be made as one piece of silicone. There is nothing to snap off, and no seam for milk to sit in, which matters when it has to be washed by hand every night.

With the shape settled, I designed the mould to be 3D printed. This was the hardest part of the project and the part I learned most from.

The mould has to come apart cleanly, so I ran the split line straight through the middle of the wings. That keeps the seam away from the surface that does the sealing, where any leftover material would give milk a path out.

The tool is three parts, two halves and a separate core forming the inside of the cap. The core was the difficult one. It has to sit in the same place every pour with nothing holding it from outside, or the cap comes out with one wall thicker than the other. Several printed trials got it seating properly, tested on small coupons first so I wasn’t spending hours of print time to find out a shape didn’t work. Four M4 screws clamp and align the halves, doing both jobs at once.

The gate sits at the centre of the top and the silicone is poured from above, so it spreads outwards evenly and reaches the wings, hooks and strap at roughly the same time. An off-centre gate would fill the near side first and leave the far features finishing last, which is where short fills happen.

The first cap I made had two problems. Squeezing the wings bent the flat top instead of stretching the ring, so the hooks never moved and the cap had to be yanked off. The top was thinner than the ring, so it was the part that gave way first. For the second iteration, I made the top thicker so the ring became the softer of the two.

The silicone also stopped short of the far end of the strap channel, so the locking tab never formed. That end of the channel is a dead end at the finish of a long thin path, and the air in it had nowhere to escape. It meant I couldn’t test the strap at all on this prototype.

The second cap worked the way it was meant to. Squeezing the wings pulled the ring out of round and the hooks lifted clear, so the cap came off without being forced. The thicker top did exactly what the change was meant to do. It stopped being the part that gave way, so the squeeze went into the ring where it belonged.

The thicker top also improved the seal, which I had not planned for. On the first cap the top flexed under the pressure of a full bottle held upside down, and any movement there works against the lip that does the sealing. Stiffening it meant the lip stayed put and held its shape under load. Both caps passed the upside-down test, but the second one held with more margin rather than just scraping through.

The strap came out complete and I could finally test it. It reached around the bottle handle, the tab locked into its slot, and the cap stayed with the bottle when it was off.

What I took from it

The failed cap taught me more than the working one. I’d written a requirement saying the cap must open in under three seconds with one hand, and the broken cap did exactly that, because you could still pull it off. Nothing in writing said it had to open by the mechanism I’d built, so a total failure passed the written test. I now write requirements around how something has to work, not just how well it performs.

Skills used. SolidWorks modelling and manufacturing drawings, tolerance analysis from measured samples, design for manufacture, three-part mould design, 3D printing, two-part silicone moulding, prototype testing.
