VEX Project

University of Auckland mechatronics paper

A VEX robot programmed to run a delivery task inside a scaled warehouse mat. The robot starts at the charging station, drives to a fragile payload, lifts it, follows a brown line through the aisle, places the payload in the drop off zone and returns to the charging station, all inside 120 seconds and without touching the stock shelves. I worked in a team of two and owned the motion control layer, the PI controlled straight driving routine that every other movement in the run sits on top of, the ultrasonic positioning function built from it, and the routine that drives forward until a light sensor finds the line. In practice the robot ran the full task three times in around 50 seconds each with maximum placement accuracy. Under assessment lighting the line detection failed and the final score averaged 70 points.

Purpose

The robot has no map. Everything it knows about where it is comes from two wheel encoders, one ultrasonic sensor and three light sensors, and a run is roughly twenty commanded moves in series. An error of a few millimetres in the second move is still there in the twentieth, so the only thing that makes the sequence work is each individual move landing on its target and stopping there. That is what my functions had to do. Rather than write each move separately, I built one distance controller that all of them call, so any improvement to how the robot drives straight improved every step of the run at once.

The mat is a scaled dairy warehouse. The robot starts at the charging station in one corner, the payload sits on a stand at a known location, and the drop off zone sits at the far end of an aisle marked by a brown line with a black line at either end of it. The walls and the stock shelves are hard boundaries, so any drift into them ends the run. Placement is scored on how close the payload lands to the target, which is what makes the accuracy of each individual move worth engineering rather than just getting the robot to the right general area.

The full run reads as a chain of moves. Reset the encoders, raise the arm to calibrate, drive until the ultrasonic sensor reads 450 mm from the payload, lower the arm, close the last 220 mm and lift. Reverse to line up with the brown line, turn 90 degrees, creep forward until a sensor finds the black line, follow the brown line to the drop off zone, place the payload, then navigate back to the finish area on encoders alone. Nothing in that chain corrects itself later, so the routine is only as good as the individual moves underneath it.

Every move in the run calls the same driving function. It counts wheel encoder ticks, converts them to millimetres travelled, and adjusts motor power based on how far the robot still has to go, easing off as it closes on the target and holding a small correction at the end so it settles on the exact distance rather than near it. A second correction runs alongside it, comparing the left and right wheels and shifting power to whichever one has fallen behind, which keeps the robot square in the aisle instead of drifting into the shelves.

The change that made the biggest difference was the simplest. Starting the motors at full power made the wheels slip, and a slipping wheel counts ticks the robot never travelled, so the robot thought it had gone further than it had. Ramping the power up over the first 300 milliseconds of every move fixed it, and repeat runs started landing in the same place.

Two moves in the run cannot be written as a fixed distance because the robot does not know exactly where it is starting from. Both are built on the same function. One reads the ultrasonic sensor, works out how far the robot has to travel to sit at a set distance from the payload or the wall, and drives that. The other creeps forward slowly while watching all three light sensors, and stops the moment one of them finds the line, slowly on purpose so the robot stops on the line rather than coasting over it.

The robot worked in practice and failed under assessment. It stopped detecting the black line at the start of the line following section, and where it did detect it, it ran on past the end of the line. We rechecked the calibration values, found them correct, lowered the thresholds and widened the ranges, and the robot ran the mat again. The same failure came back in the second assessment window, and with the clock going the only option left was to reset the robot and keep our own shadows off the mat.

Mapping the bands afterwards showed why the tolerances were the wrong shape. On the right sensor, brown sat at 1800 with a range of 200 and black at 2500 with a range of 300, which leaves everything between 2000 and 2200 reading as neither colour. The middle sensor had the opposite problem, a 50 unit overlap where a reading could count as both. Any lighting shift that pushed a reading into that gap stalled the run.

What I took from it

The code was written against the light levels on the bench where we tested it, and nothing in it noticed when those light levels changed. A threshold that holds on one mat under one set of lights is a calibration, not a design. The same applies to the ultrasonic function, which takes one reading at the start of a move and trusts it for the rest. Both worked in every test we ran, and both are single points of failure that testing on the bench was never going to find.

Skills used. Embedded C, closed loop motion control, encoder odometry, ultrasonic and light sensor integration, sensor calibration, systematic debugging under time pressure.

What I would do differently

Set the brown and black bands adjacent on every sensor so no reading falls between them, and calibrate each sensor separately rather than sharing one value across three.

Compare the sensor readings against each other rather than against fixed values, so a change in ambient light moves all three together and the robot keeps working.

Load several versions of the routine with different calibration values before the assessment, so lighting on the day is not a single point of failure.
